Key Takeaways

Between the Republican reconciliation law’s July 2025 enactment and July 2026 (the last month with data on all states):

SNAP participation is down by 5.5 million people, or 13 percent, in USDA data.
SNAP participation has dropped in every state except Alaska, including by close to 5 percent or more in 48 states, nearly 10 percent or more in 26 states, and close to 20 percent or more in 8 states, in USDA data.
SNAP participation has fallen to 36.1 million people, its lowest point in 17 years. It is well below the pre-pandemic level of nearly 37 million in February 2020.
The last time there was such a steep decrease in participation in this short period of time (other than temporary spikes following natural disasters) was nearly three decades ago, after Congress enacted very deep cuts to SNAP (then the Food Stamp Program) in 1996. SNAP participation dropped by 13 percent (3 million people) in the 12 months from April 1997 to April 1998.
In the 28 states with publicly available data or that have shared unpublished data, the number of children receiving SNAP has fallen by 1.5 million since July 2025. It’s very likely that nationwide, the number of children receiving SNAP has dropped by about 2 million since July 2025.
Unemployment nationwide has been flat at 4 percent since July 2025, BLS data show. At best, it’s down in 26 states and the District of Columbia by a little over a percentage point. It’s very unlikely that reduced need is driving the decline in SNAP participation.

These participation declines began before some of the major changes from the harmful 2025 Republican reconciliation law were fully in effect. We expect these harms to grow as states fully implement eligibility restrictions and take actions to lower their error rates. 

The harmful Republican reconciliation law enacted July 4, 2025, poses great harm to families, including higher food costs, poverty, and hunger. It makes deep cuts to federal funding for food assistance through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), shifting significant new costs to states. It dramatically expands SNAP’s already harsh and ineffective provision taking away people’s benefits for not meeting a work requirement. And it ended eligibility for many people with a lawful immigration status. 

We estimate that SNAP participation nationwide fell by 5.5 million people (13 percent) between the law’s July 2025 enactment and July 2026, based on the latest month of data from the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA).

One year following the law’s enactment, the declines are especially pronounced in some states. SNAP participation fell in Arizona by nearly 51 percent from July 2025 to July 2026. The decline is 45 percent from July 2025 to September 2026, using more recent state data. The caseload declines are 20 percent or more in Arizona, Florida, Illinois, Louisiana, Nebraska, Nevada, and Oklahoma, and — using more recent state data — in Indiana, Kansas, and Texas.

The declines started before the harmful 2025 Republican reconciliation law’s enactment, suggesting factors at play in addition to that law. But in almost all states, declines in SNAP participation accelerated after the 2025 Republican reconciliation law, and we expect that trend to continue. (Some states have had very small increases in participation in recent months, but remain substantially below their level in July 2025.) Use the map and charts below to see details.

In the 28 states with available data, the number of children receiving SNAP food assistance has fallen by 1.5 million since July 2025. Put another way, children account for close to half of the nearly 3.7 million people who are no longer receiving SNAP in those states. (See table.) It’s very likely that nationwide, the number of children receiving SNAP has dropped by about 2 million since July 2025.[1]

Starting in October 2027, most states will have to pay between 5 and 15 percent of SNAP benefit costs, totaling hundreds of millions of dollars a year in many states. And the amount a state will have to pay will be based on fiscal year 2025 or 2026 error rates. The magnitude of the cost shift and the urgency surrounding error rates incentivized states to take drastic measures to reduce their payment error rates quickly and cut program costs, even if it meant delaying or improperly denying benefits to eligible people. We’re likely seeing some of those initial effects in SNAP participation data.

In addition, the harmful 2025 Republican reconciliation law expanded SNAP’s already harsh and ineffective work requirement, extending it to older adults up to age 65 and to parents and other caregivers of children age 14 and older. It also substantially narrowed states’ ability to waive the time limit for areas with few job opportunities and eliminated exemptions for veterans, people experiencing homelessness, and former foster youth. Research consistently shows the work requirement cuts many people off SNAP without increasing employment or earnings, indicating that many of these recipients are likely to be cut off from SNAP benefits as states ramp up implementation.

We are tracking state SNAP participation data to identify trends likely attributable to the 2025 Republican reconciliation law. This tracker shows the most recent federal data on SNAP participation and often more recent data from state agencies administering the SNAP program, as well as unemployment data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). This tracker is initially focused on SNAP participation and will be regularly updated as new data become available. Additional indicators may be added over time.

The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimates that the harmful 2025 Republican reconciliation law cuts SNAP by nearly $187 billion through 2034, the largest cut to the program in history, and that more than 2.4 million people in a typical month would be cut from SNAP. When also including people losing a substantial amount, but not all, of their SNAP benefit, we estimated based on CBO’s figures that 4 million people in a typical month will lose out, once the changes are fully implemented. The actual drops in SNAP participation in the first year after the law’s enactment have already far exceeded CBO’s estimates.

The Harmful 2025 Republican Reconciliation Law’s Impact on SNAP Administration 

Several of the harmful 2025 Republican reconciliation law’s provisions directly eliminate eligibility for SNAP, including the expansion of the work requirement to new groups of recipients, the significant limitations on work requirement waivers for areas with higher unemployment, and new restrictions that take SNAP away from people with certain lawful immigration statuses. 

The 2025 Republican reconciliation law is also substantially influencing how state agencies administer SNAP. To minimize the amount they will be required to pay under the reconciliation law’s cost-shift provisions, states are incentivized to adopt policies that will reduce error rates quickly. Error rates are measures of states’ over- and under-payments in SNAP, mostly resulting from unintentional mistakes by state agencies and households, not fraud. The states’ error rates in fiscal years 2025 and 2026 will determine how much they pay in fiscal year 2028, the first year the cost-shift provision is in effect.

Many of the policies the states are already adopting in an effort to lower error rates are likely to result in eligible households losing SNAP food assistance because of administrative burdens. This can include policies such as requiring more frequent verification of income and expenses, which may make it more difficult for eligible households to access benefits or result in processing delays. Unfortunately, given the way SNAP error rates are calculated, wrongly denying or delaying benefits to an eligible household is not considered an “error.”

SNAP participants in some states already were facing long application delays and call center wait times before the 2025 Republican reconciliation law’s enactment. The law’s short implementation timeline and cut to the federal investment in state administrative expenses are making it hard for state agencies to adjust their operations. As a result, SNAP participation declined faster over the past year than expected from the law’s implementation, resulting in increased poverty, food insecurity, and hunger.

SNAP participation may have dropped in part due to the harmful 2025 Republican reconciliation law, even before some of its major provisions have taken effect. Larger-than-expected participation declines, for example, may indicate situations where states are implementing policies in an effort to reduce error rates that have the effect of broadly reducing SNAP enrollment. Other constraints, such as staffing shortages, may also be at play. SNAP also faced an unprecedented disruption during the government shutdown late in 2025, during which the Trump Administration illegally refused to deliver benefits.

Changes in SNAP participation are driven by changes in the number of people eligible for the program and the share of eligible people who successfully enroll. SNAP responds to changes in poverty and the economy. As unemployment increases, poverty increases and more people become eligible for SNAP benefits. States with rising unemployment may see an increase in demand for food assistance. States with falling SNAP participation while unemployment is rising or holding steady may indicate that an increasing number of eligible families cannot access SNAP to help keep food on their table. Data from the Urban Institute indicate that food insecurity remained high in 2025, just as states began to implement deep cuts to SNAP and health care coverage. Some short-term indicators of economic strain or uncertainty, such as the number of people seeking food assistance at food pantries and food banks and the volume of calls to the 211 network requesting help accessing food assistance, have risen significantly over the last year.

Chart Notes

USDA SNAP participation data may include disaster assistance (D-SNAP). USDA provided D-SNAP in the fall of 2024 to Georgia, Florida, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Virginia due to hurricanes Helene and Milton; to Louisiana due to hurricane Francine; and to New Mexico due to severe storms. It provided D-SNAP to California in February 2025 due to wildfires, and Kentucky and West Virginia in the spring of 2025 and Arkansas and New Mexico in the summer of 2025, all due to severe storms. States do not often include disaster SNAP in the data they post. 

USDA and BLS routinely revise historical data when a new month of data are released. As a result, the data in this tracker may change slightly as the tracker is updated over time. The most recent month of USDA data are usually preliminary. State agency data may be preliminary and subject to later revisions. 

State SNAP participation trends may show some anomalies due to irregularities in the federal or state agency data.

USDA data for North Dakota show an anomalous blip in October 2025 (so we replaced data for that month with participation data verified through the state agency). State agency SNAP participation data for Ohio appear to be misreported for the month of October 2025, so we have omitted them. For Minnesota, the number of people the state reports includes a small number of people who receive state-funded food benefits. In addition, Minnesota, under unique authority known as MFIP, combines SNAP food benefits with cash assistance under the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families program for some families. 

Puerto Rico does not participate in SNAP. It receives a block grant for nutrition assistance.

Map the Meal Gap 2026

According to Feeding America’s Map the Meal Gap 2026 report, 649,820 people, or 1 in 7 neighbors, across Second Harvest Food Bank of Central Florida’s seven-county service area are experiencing food insecurity. This includes 178,020 children, or 1 in 5 kids. The 2026 estimates are based on 2024 data.

Food insecurity increased across Central Florida. Overall food insecurity rose in all seven counties, and child food insecurity increased by at least one percentage point in every county.

Who Is Most Impacted

Hunger affects people of all backgrounds, but some groups experience it at higher rates. Across Central Florida, 1 in 5 kids are experiencing food insecurity. Rates are highest in Marion, Volusia, Osceola and Orange counties.

Food insecurity affects 27% of Black residents, 18% of Hispanic residents and 11% of white, non-Hispanic residents across the region. These disparities reflect broader barriers to opportunity that can make it harder for individuals and families to access the food and resources they need.

Among older adults in the Orlando-Kissimmee-Sanford metro area, 5.2% of seniors ages 60+ and 6.5% of adults ages 50–59 experience food insecurity.

The Cost of Hunger

High food and household costs continue to put pressure on family budgets. Central Florida’s annual meal gap is now 123.4 million meals, a 10.6% increase from the previous year. Orange County alone accounts for 42.8 million meals, nearly one-third of the regional gap.

At the same time, 38.9% of Central Floridians experiencing food insecurity may not qualify for SNAP based on household income alone. This estimate does not include people who may lose eligibility because of more recent federal policy changes to SNAP.

What This Means for Central Florida

These findings help Second Harvest understand where hunger is increasing and where resources are needed most. By using local data to guide food distribution, school partnerships, nutrition programs and other hunger relief efforts, Second Harvest can better respond to the needs of neighbors across Central Florida.

Map the Meal Gap is an annual study by Feeding America that provides detailed, localized data on food insecurity across the United States.

Since 2011, Feeding America has produced the Map the Meal Gap study, providing estimates of local food insecurity and food costs on an annual basis to better understand people and places facing hunger and to inform decisions and actions that will help us achieve our mission.

National Risk Index Data
English

The National Risk Index dataset helps illustrate the United States communities most at risk for 18 natural hazards which are avalanche, coastal flooding, cold wave, drought, earthquake, hail, heat wave, hurricane, ice storm, inland flooding, landslide, lightning, strong wind, tornado, tsunami, volcanic activity, wildfire, and winter weather. It was designed and built by FEMA in close collaboration with various stakeholders and partners in academia; local, state and federal governments; and private industry. The National Risk Index December 2025 v1.20 data is now available through the Resilience Analysis and Planning Tool (RAPT) or through data downloads from this page.

The National Risk Index data leverages available source data for natural hazard and community risk factors to develop a baseline risk measurement for each United States county and U.S. Census tract.

Visit the National Risk Index Data website for more information, including technical documentation, version update documentation, and a user guide.

National Risk Index for Natural Hazards
English

The National Risk Index dataset provides information for communities most at risk to 18 different natural hazards. It offers a baseline risk measurement for expected annual loss, social vulnerability and community resilience at the Census tract or county level. The data helps to validate, measure and better understand your community’s natural hazard risk.

The latest National Risk Index data is available for download on OpenFEMA.

December 2025 v1.20 National Risk Index Data Now Available

The National Risk Index v1.20 data is now available through the Resilience Analysis and Planning Tool (RAPT). RAPT is the single entry point for users to access the National Risk Index data alongside other publicly available infrastructure, hazard and population data.

This update includes:

Graphic
An overhaul of the tsunami hazard using state/territory and U.S. Geological Survey data.
An overhaul of the inland flooding hazard to incorporate pluvial as well as fluvial flood hazards.
Incorporation of newly published high-resolution U.S. Geological Survey landslide susceptibility and frequency data.
Expansion the geographic extent of the earthquake hazard to the Pacific territories and the incorporation of new U.S. Geological Survey probabilistic seismic data.
